Research design - not a functioning robot

THE NEXT ROBOT HAS TO EARN EVERY NUMBER.

The humanoid work is paused. ATH is investigating a racket-bearing robotic arm on a driven wheeled base. The design target is up to 60 m/s racket-head speed, alongside broad on-court shot coverage, fast serves and high-spin groundstrokes. That speed is a requested requirement, not measured ATH performance. The hardware, its physics model and those abilities are not validated.

MEASURED PRECEDENT

ESTHER, not ATH

A published tennis robot mounted a 7-degree-of-freedom, cable-driven Barrett WAM arm and racket on a motorized tennis wheelchair. Cable drive is not the same as the QDD architecture under consideration. The researchers measured 10 m/s pre-impact racket-head speed and 1.42 m/s² autonomous base acceleration. In ball-machine court trials, 66% and 53% of shots were returned successfully in the two reported setups (15 trials each). The 60 m/s ATH design target is six times ESTHER's measured 10 m/s racket-head speed. This ratio compares targets, not proven capabilities. Those results belong to ESTHER, not our proposed robot. Read the paper.

OPEN DESIGN

Arm + driven base

Candidate layout: a powered wheeled chassis, a multi-joint racket arm, physical racket and stringbed contact, ball tracking and an inverse-kinematics planner. No arm, motor, gear ratio, wheel, power system or sensor stack has been selected as a verified ATH build. A component's catalog limit does not prove an assembled robot can perform a shot.

60 m/s drivetrain check: candidate chain fails

Verdict for the tested off-the-shelf QDD stack: no-go. Even an optimistic planar lower-bound model needs joint torques above all three selected peak catalog values to reach a 60 m/s racket tip. A custom drivetrain and a verified, full-chain dynamics solve are needed before ATH can claim this target. This does not prove that every off-the-shelf actuator arrangement or every custom design is impossible; it rejects only the documented candidate under the stated test.

Reference geometry, not a selected ATH design: the Barrett WAM 7-DOF DH table gives a 0.55 m upper arm and 0.30 m forearm; the Wilson Blade 98 listing gives a 0.685 m / 323 g strung racket. For a straight 3R planar swing, the shoulder, elbow and wrist lever arms to the far tip are approximately 1.535, 0.985 and 0.685 m. The 27-inch racket is below the ITF 73.7 cm maximum. Actual base placement, shoulder height, joint reach for serve contact, spin axis and court coverage are not yet defined and must be checked in 3D IK, not inferred from this slice.

48 V catalog stack: AK10-9 V2 KV100 shoulder - 960 g, 9:1, 421 rpm rated, 18 Nm rated / 48 Nm peak; AK80-6 KV100 elbow - 485 g, 6:1, 603 rpm rated, 6/12 Nm; AK60-6 V3 KV80 wrist - 380 g, 6:1, 490 rpm rated, 3/9 Nm. The first two vendor tables label inertia without specifying its reference frame; we have not reflected those figures through gearing. The AK60 table calls its 243.5 g cm² value rotor inertia, but even this value is excluded from the optimistic lower bound.

Method: constant nonnegative joint acceleration from rest, straight planar Jacobian v_tip = [1.535, 0.985, 0.685] dot q̇. The inertial mass matrix sums the 0.323 kg racket as a point mass at 1.18 m (its real distributed inertia is not yet measured), a 0.380 kg wrist module at 0.85 m, and a 0.485 kg elbow module at 0.55 m. These placements and the 1.5/1.5/2.0 rad sweep caps are test assumptions, not measured robot properties. A linear program minimizes the largest fraction of catalog peak torque, constraining each joint to its catalog rated speed and the assumed sweep. With all peak torques enforced instead, the mathematical tip-speed ceiling is 18.3 m/s in 0.15 s, 20.1 m/s in 0.20 s; these are upper bounds under this assumption set, not predicted ATH speeds. Missing links, fixture, moving cable/gear inertia, impact, compliance and loaded torque-speed/thermal curves could worsen the result. Do not mix a catalog peak torque with a rated-speed point and call it an attainable operating point.

Changing the swing time, joint ranges, distribution of racket mass or base motion changes the bound, but it cannot turn this simplified test into proof of a 60 m/s build. Next design gate: source a complete drivetrain and mechanically measured arm/racket inertias; solve 3D IK and inverse dynamics through contact, then verify coupled torque-speed, energy, power, current, bus sag, thermal duty cycle, base stability and measured racket/ball impact.

Variant: add a rotating torso

It helps, but this published four-axis stack does not reach 60 m/s in the stated test. A powered torso can add racket-tip velocity and permit a longer kinetic-chain wind-up. It also must transmit and accelerate all distal mass. Moving motors proximally, as in cable or remote actuation, may lower the moving inertia, but it does not itself prove any 60 m/s shot.

Scenario geometry: the preceding Barrett-manual 0.55 m upper and 0.30 m forearm, the strung Blade 98 0.685 m / 0.323 kg racket, and an assumed, not measured ATH 0.25 m horizontal offset from trunk yaw to shoulder. The Blade listing gives a 0.330 m strung balance point from its butt. With the straight planar axes at 0/.25/.80/1.10 m, the far-tip lever arms are 1.785/1.535/.985/.685 m for torso/shoulder/elbow/wrist. This geometry does not establish serve contact height or full-court reach; neither the mounting height nor the base envelope is selected.

Tested 48 V stack: CubeMars AKE90-8 KV35 torso (1.4 kg, 8:1, 55 Nm rated / 170 Nm peak, 120 rpm rated / 210 rpm no-load, external driver needed), with the same AK10-9 V2 KV100 shoulder (48 Nm peak), AK80-6 KV100 elbow (12 Nm peak) and AK60-6 V3 KV80 wrist (9 Nm peak). This is a comparison, not a hardware selection. The torso's 1.4 kg motor is assumed mounted at the trunk axis and stationary with respect to that yaw; a different mount changes its inertial load.

Extending acceleration 0.15 to 0.20 s reduces this optimized torque requirement by roughly 12% torso, 14% shoulder, 18% elbow and 19% wrist, but leaves shoulder/elbow/wrist far above their respective peak ratings. With all four peak torques enforced, the optimistic contact-speed bound is 18.3 m/s at 0.15 s, 20.1 m/s at 0.20 s, 23.9 m/s at 0.30 s, and 31.2 m/s at 0.50 s; by 0.50 s all axes hit the assumed sweep caps and a longer window does not help this constant-acceleration motion. The short-window torque-capped optimizer assigns near-zero torso speed because the distal joints are the bottleneck, not because torso rotation is useless in every trajectory.

Method and limits: a constant-acceleration, same-direction planar 4R sweep from rest; a linear program minimizes the maximum fraction of catalog peak torque to hit 60 m/s and separately maximizes tip speed within peak torque, catalog rated-speed and assumed sweep caps. The mass matrix uses the racket as a 0.323 kg point at its stated 0.330 m balance location from the butt, 0.380 kg wrist motor at the wrist, 0.485 kg elbow motor at the elbow, and 0.960 kg shoulder motor at the shoulder. These point placements are favorable test assumptions, not a measured mechanism. Arm links, torso structure, mounting hardware, motor rotor/gear inertia, racket's distributed moment, impact load, cable losses and compliance are omitted. These outputs are conditional model results, not demonstrated racket speeds or global upper bounds; peak catalog torque is not proven available at the listed rated-speed points. A 3D timed kinetic-chain motion, joint limits, off-axis load paths, stability, thermal/current limits and measured impact are still needed.

Remote-actuation route: putting larger motors near the trunk and transmitting torque to distal joints can remove motor-body mass from the shoulder-to-wrist moving chain. The backdrivable cable-driven Barrett WAM and ESTHER's 10 m/s experiment are real precedent, not proof of ATH's six-times-higher target. A cable design must publish or measure its routing, drum radii, reduction, stretch, friction, tension, fatigue and impact loads before running a credible full-dynamics solve. No catalog-verifiable combination checked here closes the 60 m/s gate.

Next workstream: move the motors off the arm

Remote actuation is the design route, not a verified 60 m/s solution. Mount motor bodies at the base or trunk, transmit joint torque with pre-tensioned cables, and make distal links light. This removes motor-body mass from the moving arm but introduces cable loads, friction, stretch and routing. The first sourced motor-and-ratio example below still fails the target, even under favorable assumptions.

Barrett's WAM inertial specification gives concrete cable hardware: Sava SN2047 1.1176 mm cable around a 9 mm motor drum to a 33 mm intermediate pulley (first stage 3.667:1); a second stage uses SN2054 1.3716 mm cable, an 11 mm intermediate pinion and a 126 mm base pulley, 84.75 mm differential-input pulley or 52 mm elbow pulley. The actual WAM ratios are 42:1 for base, 28.25:1 for shoulder differential inputs and 18:1 elbow, with output-reflected drive inertias 0.205/0.094/0.094/0.0346 kg m² for its first four drives. These are published values for that arm, not a free massless transmission for ATH. The WAM cable-maintenance guide documents pre-tensioning and cable routing constraints. ESTHER measured 10 m/s with a WAM on a powered wheelchair; it does not validate 60 m/s.

Maxon EC-i 52, 48 V, 420 W, part 633919 is a real off-chain motor lead: 752 g body, 1.02 Nm nominal torque at 3990 rpm, 4.83 Nm stall at zero speed, 5090 rpm no load and 170 g cm² rotor inertia. A hypothetical lossless 33/9 = 3.667:1 cable on each axis yields 3.74 Nm nominal joint torque, 17.71 Nm joint stall and 0.000229 kg m² reflected rotor inertia per joint. This is not an assembled or qualified Maxon-WAM drivetrain; a matched encoder, drive electronics, pulley mount, cable rating and safe tension have not been selected. The stall point is not sustainable and cannot occur at high speed.

Open 60 m/s gate: choose a higher-power motor and compatible controller with a measured torque-speed/current/thermal curve at the actual bus voltage; specify cable or belt diameter, material, pulley radii, strength, wrap, pre-tension, path friction, efficiency, compliance and fatigue; measure link/racket/fixture inertias and base stability. Only then run a time-resolved 3D IK + inverse-dynamics solve through a feasible swing window, check every joint's simultaneous torque/speed/power and cable tension, and validate contact with a real ball/stringbed. No candidate checked here has passed those gates. The same court and ball machine remain the planned sim environment once this design closes on paper.

How the model earns trust

  1. Choose exact hardware revisions, acquire manufacturer curves and measure the assembled arm, base, wheels, racket and ball. Trace each parameter to a source or an experiment.
  2. Implement full rigid-body dynamics, wheel-court contact, three-dimensional ball flight and spin, stringbed/frame contact and inverse-kinematics shot planning within measured actuator envelopes.
  3. Check numerical convergence, collisions, energy and impulse accounting. Validate the full racket-head speed time history and measured ball exit speed/spin, rather than deriving either from one peak tip speed. Hold out real trajectories, impact locations, surfaces and shot types before claiming transfer to hardware.

An IK solution is only a reachable pose. It does not prove the arm can get there in time, survive the load or make the ball land. Speed, acceleration, torque, power, tire grip and contact tests must all pass.
